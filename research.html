Job Market Paper

Platform Finance
This draft: October 2026
PDF

Platforms increasingly partner with banks to finance users, but partnership structures differ across platforms and over time. We model a capital-constrained, informed platform that partners with a capital-rich, uninformed bank, choosing between intermediation (on-lending bank funds) and information provision (sharing borrower data). The tension is that the platform internalises network externalities from on-platform trade but the bank does not, and the degree of this conflict is endogenous to the partnership choice. Our main result is a regime switch in borrower pool size: information provision, if sufficiently credible, is optimal for small pools and intermediation for large pools, and the switching threshold increases with this credibility. Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) buyer financing makes information provision in merchant financing more attractive when the BNPL provider places greater value on enabled trades.

Presented at: EEA-ESEM, Econometric Society North American Summer Meeting, Bank of Italy–Bocconi–EIEF–CEPR Conference on Financial Stability and Regulation, Asian Finance Association, IBEFA Summer Meeting, Edinburgh Financial Technology Conference, Benelux Corporate Finance Days, Dutch Economists’ Day, Bundesbank–Frankfurt Summer School, University of Amsterdam

Working Papers

Single-Stock ETFs
with Yuchou Peng
Abstract

Single-stock leveraged and inverse ETFs must rebalance their exposures daily, generating predictable buy orders in the underlying stocks at the close after prices rise and sell orders after they fall. Because these funds rebalance at the closing price, their orders impose a cost on fund holders if they temporarily move that price and the move later reverses. We ask whether this cost persists even though the orders follow a public rule that lets other traders anticipate them. Using fund records and intraday data for 249 stocks from July 2022 through September 2026, we exploit variation in the size of these orders for a given stock return and relate it to closing-auction volume and subsequent returns. We find that larger predicted orders correspond to higher closing-auction volume and return reversals extending through the next afternoon. The implied annual cost is about 2.8% of assets for a +2× fund, 2.5 times the funds’ average expense ratio. Prices adjust before the close, yet anticipation does not eliminate the reversal. We present a model with risk-averse liquidity providers and gradually arriving long-term investors in which the reversal survives anticipation because liquidity providers must still bear part of the orders’ risk overnight.

Organisation of Nonbank Lending
Abstract

Banks increasingly collaborate with nonbanks, combining their cheaper funding with nonbanks’ specialised borrower information. Nonbanks, however, derive nonpledgeable ancillary benefits from lending, giving them an incentive to misreport borrower quality. Three common collaboration arrangements (lending to nonbanks, buying their loans, and lending directly on their assessments) differ in whether the nonbank retains a stake in the loans and thus appear to create different reporting incentives. We show that all three can nevertheless implement the same optimal contract, delivering identical lending outcomes and nonbank profits at a common funding cost. The contract requires no stake in the loans. Instead, it induces truthful reporting by cancelling financing for the entire borrower pool if the bank detects misreporting and by tying the nonbank’s share of the loan surplus to how much of its report the bank can confirm. Higher funding costs reduce this surplus and disproportionately harm nonbanks with smaller borrower pools, forcing them to further adjust lending to preserve truthful reporting. Because nonbanks can switch arrangements, regulation that raises the cost of one cannot increase their funding costs beyond the gap to the next-cheapest alternative.
